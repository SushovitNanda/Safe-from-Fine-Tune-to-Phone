\textbf{Context and Motivation.} Clinics fine-tune small open models and compress them for cheap hardware; their users often write in Hindi or Hinglish. For Tiny Aya Global, our model, public safety numbers cover only full-precision English with the system prompt.

\textbf{Question and Problem.} We follow it (3.35B) through that chain. Where are its refusals weakest? Does benign medical fine-tuning widen the gaps? Which components carry refusal? Can a repair without retraining restore it, and does that repair survive quantization, a stripped prompt and equal-size protection?

\textbf{Principal Ideas and Results.} We use paired comparisons, a dated pre-registration, per-language judge checks and two engines. One medical LoRA epoch doubles attack success (18.2\,\% to 36.3\,\%; $+10.9$ points on non-degenerate answers), while a token-matched control moves 1.8 points. A run-time steering vector removes about 55\,\% of this drift without measurable accuracy loss. Its effect persists at 4-bit NF4 and in llama.cpp at Q4\_K\_M and Q3\_K\_M, without the system prompt and on a simulated budget-phone stack (retention 0.79--1.16, wide intervals), at a cost in clinical over-refusal. Weight-space patching, whole-model or circuit-restricted, failed to reach the 5-point margin; our circuit-guided bit protection did no better than random bits; and we could not link the circuit map to the drift. Most fine-tuned Hinglish output is degenerate, inflating its measured harm.

\textbf{Contribution.} Per-language and quantized Hindi and Hinglish safety rates for Tiny Aya; the ClinicSafe-Hi benchmark; a cross-engine retention test; negative results for circuit-guided repair and protection; and a validity audit (degenerate output, judge failure, stop-token mismatch).
